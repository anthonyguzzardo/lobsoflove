Market research for the Courts idea · October 10, 2026 · built from four research notes in research_notes/Tennis app market and court status/

Build the Season First, Borrow the Courts

As of October 2026 no app anywhere runs a closed friend-group tennis season with character-select avatars, courts as levels with history, photo-verified match starts, ATP-style points and play-money markets, and the money in racquet sports has gone the other way: into club booking software (CourtReserve's $54M growth round, Playtomic's €65M raise and its September 2026 TIMP acquisition) and into ratings (UTR, DUPR), none of which touches a friend group (The Dink; Prowly). The nearest competition is a 2025–26 wave of solo-developer "Strava for tennis" loggers (Slice, Rackt, Twenty Two, Tennboard, Tengether) that do feeds, head-to-heads and match cards, yet none has crossed 25 App Store ratings, which says the slot is open and that logging alone does not win it (Slice; Tennboard). Live public-court status is the one part of the plan the evidence argues against as a headline: four approaches have been tried since 2017 (manual reports, geofence check-ins, cameras, camera-AI), every consumer attempt sits at 0–2 ratings, San Diego has no reservation feed to piggyback on, and UCSD's booking portal is sign-in only, so presence should be an opt-in signal on court pages among friends with a founder-seeded hourly prior, not the product (CourtCheck; UCSD policies). Golf's surviving apps all draw the same paywall line (leaderboards and friend games free, personal analytics paid at $30–100 a year), Strava converts roughly 2% of registered users, and Sleeper won fantasy by making league chat and per-league identity the product and adding money years later in separate tabs, so the tennis app should be free for the group indefinitely and treat revenue as a later, analytics-shaped add-on (Golf Pad; Sacra; Front Office Sports). The recommended first test is close to the founder's own: roster, photo-verified match log with dual confirmation, one season table and a shareable match card, three people, UCSD courts, two weeks, with the court recorded from match one and the knock, gear log, markets and kit deferred until friends log matches unprompted and post the card.

Racquet capital bought clubs and ratings, not friend groups

The incumbents the founder listed from memory are real, but the map has shifted in two ways since that doc was written. Playtomic is bigger and closer than assumed: 4.7M players, 6,500+ clubs, 66+ countries, roughly €240M in 2024 gross bookings, 102 US padel clubs (26% of the US total, effectively tied with Playbypoint), and since March 2026 an official US pickleball entry through a DUPR integration that brings its "Open Matches" stranger-matchmaking to American pickleball (DUPR blog; Padel Browser). Yet its product is organised around the club, not the player. An October 7, 2026 critique put it precisely: Playtomic "has the liquidity… booking and paying just work," but "using Playtomic feels like work. The product is organised around the club, while I arrive with a question about me," with no view of who is attending and no social context (Guzhikov Substack). US App Store reviewers call its level algorithm "the worst possible system imaginable" and complain of auto-cancelled open plays and 5% fees (App Store). Its 2025–26 acquisitions (Sportclubby, TIMP) are all club-side SaaS, so a friend-group season is outside anything it is building (Padel Cluster).

The ratings companies are the second pillar and the same story. UTR Sports has about 1.7M activated ratings, a $11.99/month "Power" subscription that reviewers say "does nothing," Flex Leagues that guarantee four matches in five weeks against strangers in a rating band, and a December 2024 purchase of PicklePlay (150,000 users, 32,000 courts) (Tennis Australia; UTR App Store; GlobeNewswire). DUPR, controlled by Agassi, Kass and Raine since January 2024, is the official pickleball rating for UPA, USAP, PPA and MLP, and it spent 2025 absorbing a backlash over "controversial algorithm changes" in which players lost points for wins and saw retroactive swings (DUPR blog; DUPR App Store). Two details matter for this plan. DUPR has carried Lucra's real-money SDK since August 2023, letting players compete "for real money… or cash alternatives–court time, tickets, merchandise," which is the only live precedent for stakes on rec matches and is real-money and venue-oriented, the opposite of play money among friends (BusinessWire). And both companies are B2B2C, earning from events, leagues and API partners, which is why neither has any reason to build a closed-group ladder.

The founder's prior-art list underweights one company and omits a whole tier. Pickleheads is the biggest adjacent risk: 720,000+ registered players, 24,000+ locations, 1M+ monthly users, a 4.9-star rating from 11,000+ reviews, a July 2026 CourtReserve integration with direct DUPR score submission, "Court Chat" for players who follow a court, and automated check-in "in active development" (Pickleheads blog; Pickleheads Plus; Pickleball Union). It is organiser-led and pickleball-only, but it is one product decision away from tennis groups with court check-ins. The omitted tier is the indie wave converging on the founder's own idea: Slice (v2.6.8 on September 30, 2026; singles scoring, follow friends, a feed with "claps," head-to-head rivalry and shareable match cards, called "the Strava for Tennis" by reviewers), Rackt (rankings, Umpire Mode, King of the Court), Twenty Two (groups, skill tiers, released February 2026 with zero ratings), Tennboard (club leaderboards of 2–20 players) and a July 2025 scorekeeper that literally sells "who's really the king of the court between you and your friends" (Slice; Rackt; AppGoblin; Tennis Scorekeeper). Legacy ladder tools are worse: TennisRungs, owned by CourtReserve, has not been updated since January 2020 and holds 3.7 stars from 15 ratings (TennisRungs App Store). So the founder's "nobody found doing" claim survives with one correction: head-to-head records, rankings and shareable match cards exist in tennis today; avatars, courts with history, ATP-style seasons, photo-verified starts, markets and an in-app gear log do not. Gear tracking lives only in $2.99 utilities (RacquetTune with 459 ratings, RacketLog, StringTracker, String Master), none attached to a match log (RacquetTune; Product Hunt).

27 million players, record retention, and nobody logging a score

Demand is not the problem. Tennis reached a record 27.3M US players in 2025 (+6%), with 14.5M "core" players who play 10+ times a year and generate 93% of all 616M play occasions, and 80% of 2024 players played again, a five-year retention high (USTA 2026 report PDF). Pickleball hit 24.3M (+22.8%), with 7.48M core players, and the two sports grew together; USTA calls it "a broadening of the landscape rather than a fragmentation" (SFIA; SGI Europe). The founder's cohort is over-indexed but not where growth came from: 18–34s are about 30% of tennis players (roughly 8.2M), while adults 35+ drove nearly 95% of 2025 growth (USTA PDF p.3, 12). Southern California has the highest participation rate of any USTA section at 11.9%, and San Diego ranks ninth nationally for "tennis" search interest (USTA PDF p.10, 16–17). Garmin reported racket sports as the largest year-over-year increase in recorded activities of any category in 2025, up 67% (Garmin).

What those players do with their matches is the opening. No survey exists, but the behavioural evidence is a patchwork: Apple Watch or Garmin workouts for hours (Garmin users still rename the "Others" activity to get "tennis"), Strava sport types added in January 2023 with no court stats, spreadsheets and Notes for scores, and group chats for scheduling (Garmin Forums; Strava Stories; RecAce). The dated asks line up with the plan. A Slice reviewer on August 16, 2026: "It is an absolute heaven that this app exists, otherwise I would not be able to log my practices" (Slice App Store). Tennboard users asked for set-level win/loss tracking in 2020 and praised its "charts/stats" in January 2026 (Tennboard). A Hacker News commenter in November 2025: "I can't count the number of times they've told me about a point or a game that they wish they could have shown me… 'strava for racket sports' is a great description" (HN). The network problem is also documented: a Pickleheads organiser's "biggest difficulty is just getting my fellow players to just put the app on their phone," and a Game Maker reviewer's group "was split into 4 group chats for who could make it each week" (Pickleheads App Store; Game Maker). Because every tennis logger launched in 2025–26 is stuck under 100 ratings while Pickleheads has 11,000, distribution rather than feature set is the bottleneck, which argues for a product that works for one organiser and three friends before it works for anyone else.

The tennis-or-pickleball question resolves on this evidence: tennis first. The pickleball slot is crowded on scheduling and directories (Pickleheads, PlayTime Scheduler's 565,000 players and 2.7M sessions, Game Maker, Hey Pickle) and now on level-matchmaking (Playtomic plus DUPR), while the tennis social and logging slot has nothing above 80 ratings (PlayTime Scheduler; Hitch). The same roster, match and season model extends to pickleball later, and the 600-member downtown WhatsApp group and the deputy sheriff's OpenPlayGrid, which the October 9 research identified, remain the warm pickleball audience for that step (The San Diego Sun). Two side findings support the later layers. Racket culture is a large, review-driven niche (Tennisnerd at 58,000 YouTube subscribers, Talk Tennis with millions of posts) and a weekly player's hard goods run roughly $400–700 a year once you count a $200–300 frame, $20–75 restrings and $136 shoes, so an hours-per-racket log that produces a restring reminder is both novel and cheap (ShopRestring; RunRepeat; Tennisnerd). And the "tennis guy" identity now runs in two modes, the tenniscore aesthetic (20M+ TikTok views by Wimbledon 2025, Challengers, Sinner for Gucci) and the racket-nerd technical mode, which a card showing kit, gear and results serves at once (Hello!; Tennis.com).

Live court status has failed four ways, and San Diego has no feed to borrow

The founder's doc treats live "who is on it right now" as a feature of the court page; the evidence says that is the right altitude and that anything more ambitious has repeatedly died. No national "is this court open" layer exists. Four approaches are in the field: manual crowd reports (CourtCheck, released March 2024, 2 ratings; NearbyCourt, claiming 47 metros with no disclosed users; LiveCourt, still a waitlist; CourtSeeker, a 404), passive geofence check-ins (PickledIn, "not enough ratings"; Paddle Stack, 1 rating at 1.0 stars), live cameras (ParkViewLive, 123 privately hosted locations, no ratings), and camera-AI for facilities (VisioCourt, one named deployment at Oakwell Courts in Cary, North Carolina, sold as a 10-day pilot) (CourtCheck; NearbyCourt; PickledIn; Paddle Stack; ParkViewLive; VisioCourt). Basketball ran this experiment first: Hoop Maps jumped from 1,000 to 21,000 users in weeks after ESPN coverage in March 2017 and left no business, and Fullcourt still ships "Live Court Traffic" over 60,000 courts on a September 2024 build with 185 ratings and reviewers noting empty data (TechCrunch; Fullcourt). Each failed on the same thing: a per-venue density problem in which manual reports have nobody to report, geofences only count app users, and cameras need hardware and a host. The durable status products all paired a passive signal with a non-status business: Waze's GPS traces and ads, Google's opted-in Location History with a privacy threshold that small courts rarely cross, Surfline's 500 cameras behind a $119.99 subscription, and Waitz/Occuspace's Bluetooth and Wi-Fi sensors at roughly $600 a year per small site, sold to universities (Google Maps 101; Breakfinder; McGill Reporter).

The only availability data that scales is reservations, and San Diego has none to borrow. San Francisco's Rec.us exposes an unauthenticated API with per-court RESERVABLE/RESERVATION/OPEN slots, but even there 38 of 66 locations are walk-up, and bookings measure intent, not presence: SF replaced Spotery in 2024 precisely because it "was unable to provide a real-time check-in system" to catch no-shows (browse.sh; Tennis Coalition SF; KTVU). In San Diego, public tennis is largely run by nonprofit clubs on city land under Special Use Permits (Balboa, La Jolla, Peninsula, Barnes), booking through CourtReserve or phone behind member logins, while city pickleball is free, first-come "open play" with fixed weekly hours published as a PDF; the 2022 city inventory counted 143 tennis and 111 pickleball courts on park land (City Report No. 202; La Jolla Tennis Club; City pickleball PDF). Pacific Beach Rec's eight courts are "first-come, first-served," "always busy," and the advice is "arrive early if you actually want to play" (Club Recess). UCSD, the founder's home court, has 13 tennis and 8 pickleball courts across Muir, Warren, Coast and North Campus, bookable only by members in one-hour slots that open three days ahead at midnight, one per member per day with one free guest, through a sign-in portal that displayed "Bookings Suspended" when fetched and publishes no schedule (UCSD outdoor courts; UCSD policies; rec.ucsd.edu/booking).

So is a live public-court layer viable? As a headline product, no; as three cheaper things, yes. First, opt-in "I'm here" on a court page among friends costs nothing and has value even at three users because the audience is the roster, not the public, which sidesteps the density trap entirely. Second, a founder-seeded hourly prior (a "Popular Times" for roughly ten San Diego hubs: Pacific Beach Rec, Robb Field, Lake Murray, Morley Field, Waterfront Park, UCSD Muir and North Campus) built from a few weeks of physical logging is useful before any live report exists and is exactly what sank the earlier apps' cold start. Third, a single Occuspace-style sensor at one hub is a $600 experiment that produces a real passive signal, pending the permission questions nobody has researched (Campus Technology). The policy angle is a bonus: San Diego's pickleball advocates "hired drones to surveil the courts and took screenshots of court logs to prove many courts went unused," which shows that impartial occupancy data is contested and valuable to the city, not only to players (Voice of San Diego). A global layer is out of reach for a solo founder and unnecessary for the product; Pickleheads, Playtomic and CourtReserve's Florida "Network" beta are all circling discovery and will add check-ins before an outsider can map the country (CourtReserve).

Golf keeps social free; Sleeper made chat and identity the product

Golf is the best-documented analog because six apps with the same audience shape have converged on one business model. Across 18Birdies ($99.99/yr), Golfshot ($79.99/yr), Hole19 (€99.99/yr), Golf Pad ($29.99–49.99/yr) and TheGrint, GPS, scorecards, live leaderboards, friend games and year-in-review summaries are free, and what sits behind the paywall is personal performance: shot tracking, strokes gained, AI caddie, handicap benchmarks, green maps (Golf Pad vs 18Birdies; Hole19; 18Birdies Clubhouse). Things about us retain; things about me get paid for. Arccos shows golfers will pay about $200 a year when hardware removes the logging burden, and SwingVision shows the tennis equivalent: 20,000+ paying subscribers and $4M+ ARR at $96–300 a year for video-derived stats, still only 0.1% of US core tennis players (Arccos Air; Kingscrowd). No golf app discloses conversion or retention, so the evidence is structural, not measured. What died is instructive: GolfMatch, a social-network-first app with no utility hook, pivoted to an Instagram-for-golf feed and vanished after 2017; Zepp died with its sensor; Zwift raised prices 33% in May 2024, lost concurrent users and laid off staff twice (GolfWRX; the5krunner). The exits were all tuck-ins into B2B platforms (Golf Genius now powers ~11,000 facilities after buying Golfshot and SwingU; Lightspeed bought Chronogolf for ~$10M), which mirrors racquet sports' CourtReserve and Playtomic and warns that a consumer tennis app is not on its own a venture outcome (Golf Retailing; PitchBook).

Golf also answers the markets question. The dominant side-game pattern is "scorekeeper, not bookmaker": Skins App computes 13 games and hands settlement to Venmo or Zelle, as do Press, Beezer and Unknown Golf, so the app never touches money (Golf.com; Press Golf). That model rides on users quietly violating Venmo's terms, which prohibit "transactions involving gambling, gaming and/or any other activity with an entry fee and a prize, including games of skill" (Venmo legal). The real-money alternatives are a legal patchwork: TheGrint's Calcutta partner wagers in USDC through smart contracts, Sleeper's pick'em was legal in only 26 states as of May 2026 and was pulled from New York, and Sleeper's Kalshi-powered markets launched February 2026 under CFTC rules with deposit caps (TheGrint; RotoGrinders; Legal Sports Report). Nobody has shipped a compelling points-only side-bet layer in any sport; the market solved for real money instead, which leaves the founder's play-money "who wins Friday" with zero regulatory load and no incumbent. The October 9 research reached the same conclusion from the prediction-market side: closed-group play money with charity as the only outbound path is the shape Manifold proved and Meta's reported "Arena" does not target, though no lawyer has reviewed a charity pot in California (Wikipedia: Manifold; NPR).

Sleeper is the analog for the roster and the season. It raised $40M at a $400M valuation in September 2021 with 3M users and reached 10M users by February 2026 by making the league chat the product: "Run Your League Free Forever," GIFs, reactions and DMs inside the league, a different avatar per league so one person can be a different character in each friend circle, and collectible animated mascots (Front Office Sports; SBC Americas; Sleeper 2020 recap). Users spend 20–35 minutes per gameday, which came from live scoring plus chat during the event, the direct analog of match-day scoring plus a court-side thread. Two Sleeper lessons cut against parts of the founder's doc. Mascots originally fought on the matchup screen and Sleeper removed that in August 2023 "to reduce clutter," keeping mascots as avatars: identity stayed, animation went, so the Smash-style character select should be a profile and a card, not an effect on the score screen (Sleeper on X). And money products arrived years after the social graph locked in, in separate tabs sharing one wallet, which is the order the six layers should follow. Strava confirms the retention side of venue-as-level: Local Legends (most completions of a segment in 90 days) launched free in 2020 while KOM leaderboards went behind the paywall, clubs nearly quadrupled to 1M in 2025, and Gen Z says it will use Strava more and Instagram and TikTok the same or less (DC Rainmaker; Running Lookout). TheGrint's "King of the Course" weekly per-course leaderboard and the USGA's October 2026 GHIN Challenges (750+ per-course net leaderboards, 30,000+ golfers in year one) show venue leaderboards retain when they are historical and asynchronous, exactly where basketball's live check-ins failed (TheGrint; First Call Golf).

Conclusion

The research changes the plan in three ways. The season, not the court map, is the defensible asset: court geometry is free (OpenStreetMap already holds 500,000+ tennis court polygons, Phoenix and Colorado publish GIS inventories), directories are owned by Pickleheads, and booking belongs to clubs, so the only thing nobody can copy is the history a specific friend group writes onto a specific court (OpenTennisMap; Phoenix Open Data). Live presence flips from a feature the founder was unsure how to scale to a feature that should never try to scale beyond the roster, with a seeded prior and perhaps one sensor as the San Diego-specific bets. And the indie wave means the two-week test is also a race: a dozen tennis loggers shipped in 2025–26 and none has found distribution, so the test is not whether the features can be built but whether three people at UCSD will log matches and post the card without being asked.

The other shift is in what "winning" looks like. Racquet-sport money sits with clubs and ratings bodies, golf's consumer apps exited as tuck-ins, and Strava needed ~195M registered users to reach a 2% paid base. A friend-group tennis app is therefore best understood as the October 9 report's "closed group + ritual + play stakes" shape with a real-world activity underneath, where the first prize is a group that keeps playing and logging through a whole season, the second is the San Diego pickleball crowd on the same engine, and revenue, if it comes, is a small analytics tier or a sponsor-funded charity pot rather than a reason to build.

Recommended build order and smallest first test

The founder's "smallest credible version" is confirmed by the evidence with four adjustments. First, record the court from the very first match even though there is no courts screen yet, because court history is the asset and it cannot be backfilled. Second, drop the roster's video clips and opt-in stats from the first build; Sleeper's lesson is that identity (name, avatar, card) retains and animation is clutter, and storage cost is real. Third, replace the phone knock with photo plus dual confirmation, which is the anti-cheat and the memory; the knock can come when there is a fourth player to delight. Fourth, add a per-match thread or, simpler, make the match card so good that the existing group chat becomes the thread.

Build order. (1) Roster: name, fighter avatar, card back with favourite court; local-first, Expo. (2) Match: challenge, required photo at start, score by sets, both confirm, court required; shareable card. (3) Season: ATP-style points table for the group, head-to-head records, "court king" per court, season replay card at the end of the quarter. (4) Courts as levels: swipe-through court pages built from OSM geometry with the history already accrued, opt-in "I'm here" visible to the roster, founder-seeded hourly prior for roughly ten San Diego hubs. (5) Gear log: hours per racket from logged matches, restring reminder, AI photo identification. (6) Markets: play-money "who wins Friday" and "finishes #1," subjects opt in, no betting on your own match, charity pot only after legal review. Pickleball groups and the kit-store link slot in after (4), when a second group exists.

Smallest first test. Founder plus two friends, UCSD Muir or North Campus courts, two weeks, local-first build with a shared season table. Count four things: matches logged without the founder asking (target: every match the three actually play, at least six over two weeks), share of matches confirmed by the opponent within 24 hours (target: all), match cards posted to the group chat by someone other than the founder (target: at least half), and whether anyone opens the standings on a non-match day (the Sleeper "between gamedays" signal). A fourth person asking to be added is the pass signal for building the courts layer; three people who stop logging after week one is the fail signal, and the fix is the card and the season, not more features.

Monetization note

The golf and Strava evidence says the same thing twice: keep everything about us free forever and sell, much later, things about me. Six golf apps keep GPS, scorecards, live leaderboards, friend games and year-in-review free and charge $30–50 (Golf Pad) to $80–100 (Golfshot, 18Birdies, Hole19) a year for shot tracking, strokes gained, AI coaching and benchmark analytics (Golf Pad vs 18Birdies). Strava earns an estimated $490M at $79.99 a year, about 90% from subscriptions, with paid penetration near 2% of registered users; the indie tennis loggers charge $12–50 a year and have no evidence of conversion, and even at Pickleheads' roughly 1M monthly users a 2% conversion at $20 a year yields only about $400,000 (Sacra; Pickleheads App Store). SwingVision is the one proof that rec tennis players pay ($96–300 a year, 20,000+ subscribers), and it is for video-derived insight, which argues for integrating SwingVision rather than competing with it (SwingVision subscribe). Zwift's 2024 price hike and user loss is the caution against charging a small community early (the5krunner). For this app the realistic sequence is: free with no paywall through the first several seasons; later a $20–40-a-year personal tier (win rate by court and surface, hours by racket, season exports, SwingVision-linked stats); a sponsor-funded charity pot modelled on Strava's sponsored challenges rather than player payments; and, if groups outgrow friends, an organiser tier like Pickleheads Pro ($249.99/yr) or DartConnect's $6 per player per season paid by the league. Real money of any kind should stay off the platform: Venmo bans gambling settlement, pick'em is legal in only 26 states, and the DUPR–Lucra deal shows the real-money lane is already taken by better-capitalised players.

Avoid or integrate rather than build

Avoid building a rating authority; integrate UTR or DUPR later and, if a level is needed internally, copy Playtomic's competitive-matches-only design with a reliability score. Avoid rebuilding AI video or line calling; SwingVision is ITF-approved and already does Apple Watch challenges and auto highlights. Avoid building a court directory; use OpenStreetMap (500,000+ tennis courts, ODbL, so keep match history in a separate database keyed to OSM IDs) plus city GIS layers, and accept that Pickleheads' data is proprietary. Avoid building booking; link to CourtReserve pages, club portals and rec.ucsd.edu. Avoid a public or global live court-status product, camera networks and anything that requires per-court hardware; if you want a passive signal, one Bluetooth density sensor at one hub after checking permissions. Avoid making live presence the headline; make history the headline and presence an opt-in among friends. Avoid real-money markets, Venmo-settled side bets and crypto rails; keep play money with no cash in or out, and get a lawyer's view on the charity pot before it ships. Avoid pickleball scheduling and open-play organising; Pickleheads, PlayTime Scheduler and Game Maker own it, and the DUPR–Playtomic integration now owns level matchmaking. Avoid building kit commerce; give each roster a Bonfire or CustomInk-style store link, since friend groups fall below the six-item retailer minimums. Avoid a public feed, gamified animation on the score screen, video-clip storage on day one, hardware dependence, and any paywall before the second season. Avoid betting the match-start flow on phone-to-phone NFC, which iOS does not expose to third-party apps; photo plus dual confirmation is sufficient.

Evidence gaps

Reddit (r/10s, r/Pickleball, r/sandiego, r/padel, r/UCSD), X, TikTok, Nextdoor and the Strava Community Hub were unreachable to every researcher, so all player quotes come from App Store reviews (self-selected, mostly satisfied users), Hacker News, Garmin forums, Product Hunt and local news; there are zero first-hand "I wish an app did this" posts from San Diego players in 2025–26. Pages returning 403 or errors included GlobeNewswire, Crowdcube, Pickleheads' pricing and San Diego court pages, BusinessWire, Break Sports, Forbes (DUPR algorithm interview), Kingscrowd (SwingVision subscriber figures), NYC Parks, LA Parks, Fox5, Axios, West Seattle Blog and RealClearPolitics; figures from those sources are from search snippets or secondary reports and are flagged in the notes. No app in either sport discloses retention or free-to-paid conversion, so the monetization note rests on pricing structure, not measured behaviour.
